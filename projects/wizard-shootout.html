Wizard Shootout

A top-down arena duel inspired by Tank Trouble — but with wizards. Pick a class, bounce elemental bolts off the walls of a maze that spells reshape, grab orbs for extra power and fire off your signature spell. First wizard to win five rounds takes the match.

PlayRepo

What it is

A browser game with all its art and sound generated in code: pixel-art textures built at boot, a Web Audio synth for the effects and a procedural chiptune loop that ramps up at match point. The repo holds no binary assets, which keeps cosmetics, map themes and new classes nearly free.

  • A mutable battlefield. Ice shots frost the floor and make it slippery, fire melts it back, fire on ice bursts into vision-blocking steam, and earth orbs raise temporary walls mid-round. A round that drags past 60 seconds floods the arena with orbs.
  • Ten hand-designed maps, each with a theme, plus a map editor. Every layout is validated — closed borders, all spawns present, every floor tile reachable — so a broken map fails in CI, and the editor won't save one.
  • Extras: mutators (giant projectiles, orb rain, low cooldowns, mirror maps), an experimental fog of war, a seeded daily challenge, local stats, achievements and unlockable robes.

The classes

Seven wizards, each with a signature ability on its own key and a passive. Your own element's orb is empowered.

  • Arcanist — Blink: teleport through one wall you're facing.
  • Pyromancer — Flame Burst: an eight-way nova of burning sparks.
  • Cryomancer — Frost Ring: frost nearby tiles and slow nearby enemies.
  • Stonecaller — Breach: shatter the wall tile you're facing.
  • Stormcaller — Zap Dash: a fast dash that stuns anyone touched.
  • Warden — Reflect Ward: a bubble that reflects enemy projectiles.
  • Trickster — Scatter Dash: a short dash that fires a triple spread backward.

The numbers are tuned with a self-play harness: the Hard bot plays itself over every ordered pair of classes on three maps, stepped at a fixed rate with no rendering and seeded so a run is reproducible. After the last pass every class wins between 44 and 54 percent of its rounds, inside a target band of 42 to 58. A human playtest is still to come.

Local play

  • Against a bot that pathfinds through the maze, grabs orbs, dodges shots, uses its class signature and fires only with a clear line of sight, at three difficulties.
  • Two players on one keyboard, or a party of three or four with gamepads.
  • Survival: one or two wizards in co-op against endless, escalating waves of dark wizards.
  • On a phone, one-player, survival and online play get a floating joystick that drops under your thumb, thumb-sized fire, orb and signature buttons, and a pause button, and every menu works by tap alone. Hold it sideways: upright, the game asks you to turn it and pauses a running round. It installs to the home screen and, after the first load, plays every mode but online with no network.

Online play

Online 1v1, still labelled a prototype, runs over a WebRTC data channel between the two browsers, with no game server: the game is static-hosted. The host runs the authoritative simulation and sends snapshots about 25 times a second; the guest renders what it is told and streams its input back, so there is one authority for every collision and the two arenas can't drift apart.

Introducing the two browsers is the only awkward part of a serverless game, so the lobby offers three routes to the same connection: a five-character room code swapped through a public MQTT broker used purely as a mailbox, a QR code drawn by an encoder written in the repo, and a copy-paste code that needs nothing but the two browsers and is always on screen as the fallback. A free public TURN relay lets players behind strict NATs connect; play on the same network never touches it.

All of the gameplay is synced: any class, any built-in map, every orb, and every change to the arena on both screens. Custom maps stay local, since the other player doesn't have them, and each screen paints its own cosmetics.
