GB Car-Insurance Risk Map

An expected annual motor-insurance premium for every small area in Great Britain — 41,729 LSOAs (England and Wales) and Data Zones (Scotland) — built entirely from open data and calibrated against published price indices.

Live mapRepo

The question

Why does the same driver pay £2,000 in one postcode and £700 in another? Insurers price location aggressively but publish nothing about how. The model works out the territorial effect from the outside: it takes the open data an insurer's territory model plausibly summarises — vehicle crime, area deprivation, traffic intensity, who lives there — and regresses it against the only public ground truth, published premium indices.

It is a territorial risk proxy, not a quote engine: it uses no individual driver or vehicle details.

The data

Ten open sources, all under the Open Government Licence, put onto one geography: the 2011 census small areas that the deprivation indices, boundaries and Scottish Data Zones all share.

  • Crime: street-level vehicle crime from data.police.uk for England and Wales; Recorded Crime in Scotland through a SPARQL endpoint.
  • Deprivation: the English, Welsh and Scottish indices, ranked within each nation.
  • Traffic: about 22,000 DfT count points, as the mean daily flow within 2 km of each area.
  • Who lives there: Census 2021 (England and Wales) and 2022 (Scotland) for the young-driver share and cars per household.
  • Diagnostics on the map, not in the price: road collisions, flood extents and local-authority traffic.
  • The anchor: a 137-row panel hand-transcribed from the WTW/Confused.com quarterly price index and MoneySuperMarket's regional figures, under a strict no-invented-figures rule — every £ figure traces to a published, cited source.

The model

Ordinary least squares on the log of each area's premium relative to the national average, with area-clustered standard errors. Modelling the ratio strips out the national price level and its drift over time, leaving the effect of place; absolute pounds come back at the end by pinning to the latest published national average.

It is deliberately small, because the calibration sample honestly is: 106 matched observations across 30 areas. Every feature enters as a rank percentile within comparable groups, so no outlier can blow up its own prediction and three nations' different scales line up.

  • An evidence gate. A feature gets in only with an independent partial correlation at acceptable collinearity, or by winning a leave-one-area-out head-to-head. It has said no more often than yes: population density, flood risk and road casualties were all gated out.
  • Place and composition. Each area gets three numbers: the full premium, a place-only counterfactual with demographics held at the national median, and the uplift between them. Composition alone explains more of the spread than place alone, and that is reported as a finding.
  • Explainable to the pound. Each premium decomposes into an exact £ step per factor from a median baseline, summing to the headline figure with no residual.
  • Honest intervals. A cluster bootstrap, widened by the out-of-sample error, gives every area a low and high premium.

From the July 2026 calibration: a panel R² of 0.930, a leave-one-area-out error of £76.51, a temporal back-test error of £69.76, and a modelled premium span of £186 to £1,578 across Great Britain.

A static React and MapLibre map on GitHub Pages: search any GB postcode, see the area's premium and its per-factor breakdown. There is no server in production.

What was hard

  • Open data that wasn't a download. England's flood extents had to be requested from Defra by email and arrived over credentialed SFTP; a Welsh ArcGIS organisation was decommissioned mid-project; SEPA's API intermittently rejects complex geometries, so the ingest retries feature by feature.
  • Three nations, three scales. Crime recording differs between England and Wales and Scotland, Scottish crime arrives at council grain, and Wales has no pure crime domain in its deprivation index.
  • Geometry that breaks itself. Re-simplifying after repairing polygons once re-broke two Scottish areas, so the repair is always the last geometry step.
  • Proxies that looked right. Local-authority traffic per resident turned out to be an inverse-density proxy, and population density was always collinear; point-level traffic flow replaced both.
  • Stale premiums. Running the risk stage without recalibrating can ship stale premiums, and it has bitten before; the calibrate step now re-runs it, and a serve-consistency test guards the map against stale coefficients.

What it doesn't claim

  • It validates at postcode-area and region grain and predicts at small-area grain, so per-area figures are directionally strong rankings, not quotes.
  • Area aggregates predict area premiums; nothing here supports conclusions about individuals.
  • Wealthy-but-central areas can under-price, and Northern Ireland is excluded for want of open crime data.
